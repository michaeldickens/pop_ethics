Transparency · the full rulebook

Every conflict and every bullet

This page lists every result the Population Ethics Consistency Test can report, and the answers that trigger each. The quiz only shows you the handful that apply to your own answers; here they are all laid out so you can read through the whole of its logic.

A conflict is a set of answers that cannot all be true together. A bullet is a position that is perfectly consistent but carries a cost many find hard to accept. Every title, claim and explanation below is generated directly from the quiz’s own code; the “Appears when” box above each card is worked out by checking which answers actually make it fire.

The answers are named the way the quiz words its options. The worlds: A 100 people at 100; A+ 100 at 101 plus 100 at 25; B 200 at 64; Z 51,200 barely-good lives; K 500 at 55; K− / K+ / K++ K plus one life at −40 / 7 / 70 (Nadia); K± K with Owen down to 20 and Nadia added at 70.

Conflict · contraction inconsistency

Adding a third option changed your mind about the first two.

  1. Offered B and A alone, you judged B the better of the two.
  2. Offered A, B and Z together, you ranked A as not worse.
  3. What is best of three is still best when one of the others is taken away.

This violates Sen’s property α: if something is best in a set, it must still be best in any subset that contains it. Z’s presence cannot put A alongside B if it did not already.

Conflict · collapsing principle

Your unrankable case has a determinate one right next to it.

  1. Adding Nadia with a modest good life makes the outcome incomparable.
  2. Adding Nadia with a wonderful life makes the outcome better.
  3. A tiny improvement to one life cannot turn “unrankable” into a determinate verdict.

You judged K+ unrankable against K, while K++ — the same world with Nadia’s life going better — you ranked determinately. Between welfare 7 and 70 for her, then, lies a point where one unit of wellbeing converts “no fact of the matter” into a settled verdict. You said that cannot happen. This is Broome’s collapsing principle (Weighing Lives ch. 12), his argument that incomparability and vagueness cannot both live in one betterness ordering. Note it is a constraint on the determinacy of your ordering rather than on its content, so it is listed apart from the conflicts above — and it is contested: Carlson and others have argued the principle is too strong.
